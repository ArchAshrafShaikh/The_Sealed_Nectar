# Love first, chronology later: teaching seerah at 4 to 8

Established children's programmes, scholars' guidance and child-development research agree on the main point: at 4 to 8 the aim is attachment to the Prophet and imitation of his character, delivered as a few short single-event stories that an adult tells, repeats and follows with something to do, while full chronology and the battles wait until about 7 to 9. The app already matches the cautious end of every position on depiction and sound, because it draws no people and uses no music, and its story length and one-story-a-week rhythm fit what the programmes and the repetition research support. Three things in it run against the evidence: it is described as built for the child to use alone, things in the picture can be tapped while the story is being told, and the lesson of each story is mostly left for the child to work out. The pilot's "touch the answer to turn the page" design is close to the one tap pattern the research supports, but its questions are mostly predictions, which have no direct evidence at this age, where "why" questions do. Source Code Academy offers no seerah for children at all, and Sahil Adeem's views on the subject are known here only from episode titles and chapter headings, so they are leads, not a method. Two gaps matter most: no programme has published an independent evaluation of its seerah teaching for this age, and no study tests the app's central idea, a parent's recorded voice against any other narrator.

Quranic Tarbiyah is the only programme whose lesson anatomy could be checked against the material itself. Its ten seerah lessons run from "The Best Man Is Born" to "The Muslims Move to Madinah", each a two-page story in five to seven short illustrated paragraphs that ends with one stated lesson and a du'a in a child's voice, followed by two pages of four small activities ([Sirah & Stories L1 PDF](https://static.quranictarbiyah.com/file/QuranicTarbiyah/ebooks/Sirah-Stories-B1-1.0-web.pdf)). Singapore's national programme takes the other route: love of the Prophet and simple sunnah at 5, his titles and the cave story at 6, his family and daily routine at 7, a brief life story at 8, and chronological seerah only from 9 ([Muis aLIVE](https://www.muis.gov.sg/ie/Programmes/Kids/Kids-aLIVE)). Miraj, the nearest thing to this app, keeps its stories for ages 4 to 9 to **2 to 7 minutes**, and for its Night Journey story lists what the child will learn and questions for the family to talk about afterwards ([Miraj stories list](https://www.mirajstories.com/library/stories), [Night Journey](https://www.mirajstories.com/library/stories/the-amazing-night-journey)).

### What they share

Five practices recur. A first pass is small: the count clusters near 20 events (Learning Roots' 20 tags, the Seerah Project's 20 events, Goodword's 20 to 23 stories, IQRA's 25 topics), and Quranic Tarbiyah uses 10 because it covers only Makkah at age 5 to 6; that clustering is the researcher's reading across products, not a rule any of them states. Each story carries one moral, usually closed with a du'a, a prayer or a question turned towards the child's own life. The child's part at 4 to 6 is listening, colouring, tracing, talking and handling physical pieces, and a map or timeline is the shared device for retelling. An adult tells the story in every programme; none expects a young child to learn seerah alone, and Quranic Tarbiyah tells the adult to "tell and show the stories" from memory with voice, pace and props, not to read them aloud ([Sirah & Stories L1 PDF](https://static.quranictarbiyah.com/file/QuranicTarbiyah/ebooks/Sirah-Stories-B1-1.0-web.pdf)). And battles are largely absent below about 7.

### Where they differ

They differ on order, on hard content and on what the child does on a screen. Chronological programmes begin at the birth; character-led ones begin with qualities and habits. Quranic Tarbiyah keeps the deaths of his father, mother and grandfather, the boycott and the Year of Sadness in its book for 5-to-6-year-olds, framed as "Allah took care of him at every moment" ([Sirah & Stories L1 PDF](https://static.quranictarbiyah.com/file/QuranicTarbiyah/ebooks/Sirah-Stories-B1-1.0-web.pdf)), while Muis and Miraj choose gentle episodes such as the cave, a camel and the Night Journey. IQRA Network is the outlier in naming Hunayn and "confrontations with enemies" for a course it says starts at 5 ([IQRA Network](https://iqranetwork.com/courses/sirah/)). Miraj has children "tap, swipe and choose their way through each story" ([Miraj stories list](https://www.mirajstories.com/library/stories)), which is the kind of interaction the research below treats with caution. Publisher age labels are loose: Learning Roots gives both 7+ and 8+ for the same book on one page ([Learning Roots](https://www.learningroots.com/products/my-prophet-muhammad)).

## Source Code Academy sells no children's seerah, and Adeem's method is unrecorded in writing

The academy's catalogue for children is Arabic for ages 4 to 7 and 8 to 12, Qur'an recitation for under-12s, STEM from 7 and a teen mentorship; no course teaches seerah or prophet stories to children ([Courses](https://sourcecode.academy/courses)). The one class a 4-year-old could join is everyday Arabic vocabulary in **30-minute live sessions of six children**, taught through "pictures, games, tracing, matching, songs, role-play and simple conversations" ([Arabic for kids ages 4-7](https://sourcecode.academy/course/arabic-for-kids-4-to-7-years-old)). Seerah is taught only to adults, in Urdu, as an "analytical, mission-oriented" study of how the Prophet "built identity, community, and state" ([Islamic Foundation course](https://sourcecode.academy/course/islamic-foundation)). That is a political and strategic framing for adults, not story material for a pre-schooler. The $80 parenting workshop "How to Train Your Dragon" lists ten topic headings weighted towards teenagers and Gen Z, with nothing on storytelling, seerah or screens; its substance sits behind the paywall in video and was not seen ([Workshop page](https://sourcecode.academy/workshop/parenting-train-your-dragon)).

What Sahil Adeem says about children and seerah is known here only from podcast titles, one- or two-sentence descriptions and chapter lists. No audio or video was listened to, and no transcript was found. The single episode on the subject, "Train your Kids through Story Telling", has one sentence of description: that storytelling forms a child's psychology through heroes, fear and courage, and that the wrong story makes a child weak ([Acast, 31 Mar 2026](https://shows.acast.com/sahil-adeem-podcast/episodes/train-your-kids-through-story-telling-sahil-adeem-podcast)). Other descriptions say "children naturally follow heroes before parents realize it" and that love of the Prophet shapes identity ([Acast, 27 Aug 2026](https://shows.acast.com/sahil-adeem-podcast/episodes/hum-nabi-pbuh-se-mohabbat-kyun-karein-sahil-adeem-podcast)). Chapter headings in a long education talk read "Why Children Should Not Start School Before Age Seven" and "Why Children Should Learn Three Languages Before Age Seven" ([Acast, 4 Aug 2026](https://shows.acast.com/sahil-adeem-podcast/episodes/is-the-education-system-destroying-your-childs-brain)), and others read "Do Not Make the Qur'an Negative for Children" and "Scaring Children Creates Defeated Adults" ([Acast, 25 Jun 2026](https://shows.acast.com/sahil-adeem-podcast/episodes/the-fear-that-locked-the-muslim-mind-sahil-adeem-podcast)). "Reading Seerah Through Arabian Psychology" is likewise a chapter heading, in a talk for adults about cultural bias ([Acast, 27 Jul 2026](https://shows.acast.com/sahil-adeem-podcast/episodes/what-if-the-prophet-came-to-your-house-today-sahil-adeem)).

Read together, the headings suggest a stance: build the child around heroes, tell stories of courage, never frighten a child with religion, keep the child asking questions, put spoken language first, and treat the phone as the parents' problem. That summary is the researcher's inference from headings; the arguments, the evidence and any practical steps were not available. Which stories he would tell a small child, how, and what he would leave out is not documented anywhere in writing that was found. His claims about brain stages, "six types of intelligence" and dopamine appear without cited research and should be treated as assertions.

A parent should also weigh the source. In 2024 he said on television that 95 of 100 Pakistani women are "ignorant" and refused to apologise ([Aaj English TV](https://english.aaj.tv/news/amp/330367746)), and a police case was registered in Karachi over remarks about Sindhis ([Daily Pakistan](https://en.dailypakistan.com.pk/14-Jul-2024/sahil-adeem-faces-legal-action-over-hate-speech-against-sindhis)); the outcome of that case was not found. No source documented formal training in Islamic scholarship or child psychology, and his educational background could not be verified. Part of his story repertoire is speculative (episodes on intergalactic journeys, time travel and portals), which makes it a poor model for what to tell a 4-year-old as established seerah. The academy's own figures, such as "21,336+" active students, and its repeated testimonials are marketing with no independent check ([sourcecode.academy](https://sourcecode.academy/)). The principles that survive this (courage over fear, welcome questions, the parent as the child's first hero) can be borrowed because other sources and the research support them independently, not because of his authority.

## Scholars agree on the goal and on not drawing him; they divide on music and on hard content

Every source read puts love of the Prophet ahead of facts for a young child. IslamQA, a Salafi-leaning fatwa site, advises telling "things that she can relate to, such as the childhood of the Prophet", his time with Halimah, and the night of the Hijrah, "stories which demonstrate how Allah helped him" ([IslamQA 21215](https://islamqa.info/en/answers/21215)). A homeschooling writer on a Saudi-based publisher's blog suggests starting from the child's own interests: how he played with children, the names of his camels and horses, the toy horse of Aishah ([IIPH blog](https://blog.iiph.com/instilling-the-love-of-prophet-muhammad-sa-in-our-children)). The specific hadith about his kindness to children were not re-checked against the collections in this research, so each should be confirmed in Bukhari or Muslim before it becomes a story.

The method these sources recommend is the parent's own voice at a fixed time. One father found children's seerah books "went very, very stale" with his 6-year-old and switched to narrating from an adult reference at bedtime, toned down as he judged fit ([MuslimMatters](https://muslimmatters.org/2012/06/26/seerah-and-your-kids/)). A counsellor advises asking the child which story they want, using questions and pictures so children are "an active part of the process rather than just 'listeners'", and tying events to the child's life, for example by telling how you felt when they were born ([AboutIslam](https://aboutislam.net/counseling/ask-about-parenting/raising-positive-children/how-to-get-my-kids-to-love-the-prophet/)). Linking each story to a small sunnah and praising the child for doing it "like the Prophet did" is standard advice ([IIPH blog](https://blog.iiph.com/instilling-the-love-of-prophet-muhammad-sa-in-our-children)). These are parents and counsellors, not trials.

Three popular supports need careful handling. The "seven, seven, seven" staging formula is, according to IslamQA and Shaykh Ibn Baz, not a hadith and has no basis as one; it is passed on in books of literature as a saying of the caliph Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan, and its attribution to Ali circulates without a chain ([IslamQA 145946](https://islamqa.info/ar/answers/145946)). It can be used as a rule of thumb, not cited as prophetic. The report "Train your children to acquire three characteristics: love of your Prophet..." is graded weak by al-Albani, as IslamQA itself notes ([IslamQA 21215](https://islamqa.info/en/answers/21215)). And the two early reports usually quoted for teaching children the seerah, one from Ali ibn al-Husayn that the Prophet's campaigns were taught as a surah of the Qur'an is taught, and one from Isma'il ibn Muhammad ibn Sa'd that his father taught them the campaigns as the glorious deeds of their fathers, could only be confirmed from secondary works. The references given, al-Khatib al-Baghdadi's al-Jami' li-Akhlaq al-Rawi (2/195) and Ibn Kathir's al-Bidaya wa'l-Nihaya (3/242), come from a search summary of a commentary that could not be opened ([usul.ai](https://usul.ai/t/commentary-on-the-sealed-nectar/4)). The Arabic wording, the chains and any grading were not read from a primary edition, translations disagree on whether the first report says "we used to teach" or "we were taught", and a version attributed directly to Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas appears to be a conflation and is unverified. The reports do not give the children's ages, and "maghazi" in early usage covers the Prophet's career broadly, so they support teaching children the seerah, not teaching battle detail to a 4-year-old; that reading is the researcher's inference.

### Depiction

On the Prophet and other prophets there is effectively no mainstream Sunni dissent. The Islamic Fiqh Council in Makkah prohibits depicting him "whether it is an animated drawing or otherwise", naming children's books, and applies the same to all prophets ([IslamQA 158232](https://islamqa.info/en/answers/158232)). Most official bodies extend this to companions: the Saudi Standing Committee forbids portraying any of them, Jordan's Iftaa' Department names the Rightly Guided Caliphs, the Mothers of the Believers and the Prophet's household ([aliftaa.jo Fatwa 1891](https://aliftaa.jo/en-fatwas/1891/rss.aspx)), and Malaysia's Federal Territories Mufti rules out comics of companions "even for a good purpose of dakwah and education", with fallback conditions of incomplete, faceless figures and a storyline faithful to sound sources ([Irsyad al-Fatwa 213](https://muftiwp.gov.my/en/artikel/irsyad-fatwa/irsyad-fatwa-umum-cat/2271-irsyad-al-fatwa-siri-)). A minority view permitting lesser companions is recorded, though it reached this research only as a search summary. On animation in general there is real disagreement: AMJA permits educational cartoons for children, without saying prophets or companions may be drawn ([AMJA Fatwa 87774](https://www.amjaonline.org/fatwa/en/87774/animated-films-or-cartoons-made-for-educational-purposes)), and IslamQA holds a stricter baseline on images of living beings while still calling for Islamic cartoons ([IslamQA 71170](https://islamqa.info/en/answers/71170)).

Drawing no people at all satisfies every one of these positions and has precedent: the Islamic Texts Society's illustrated life for ages 10 to 15 made "no attempt to portray either the Prophet or his Companions" ([Islamic Texts Society](https://its.org.uk/catalogue/the-life-of-the-prophet-muhammad-paperback/)). One practical reason given for the prohibition bears on children directly: viewers "may begin to think of them in the image of the actors", and a reader reports that a film seen at 7 or 8 still supplies Hamzah's face for him as an adult ([IslamQA 158232](https://islamqa.info/en/answers/158232), [MuslimMatters](https://muslimmatters.org/2012/06/26/seerah-and-your-kids/)). Two things stay open. No fatwa was found on a light, star, silhouette or calligraphic name standing in for the Prophet; the researcher infers that the Fiqh Council's wording would cover a glowing human outline, but that is inference. And no explicit illustration policy was found for the main children's publishers.

### Music and sound

Music is disputed and the sources say so. SeekersGuidance reports the relied-upon position of all four schools as prohibiting instruments other than the duff, while telling readers not to condemn those who differ ([SeekersGuidance](https://seekersguidance.org/answers/general-counsel/listening-to-islamic-songs-with-musical-instruments/)). Egypt's Dar al-Ifta permits singing with or without instruments under conditions ([Dar al-Ifta](https://www.dar-alifta.org/en/fatwa/details/4866/musical-instruments)). Deobandi and Salafi sources are strict, and Ibn Uthaymin's allowance for cartoons is conditional on no music ([IslamQA 71170](https://islamqa.info/en/answers/71170)). All positions read accept the unaccompanied voice. No fatwa was found on natural sounds or sound effects in children's media, so "nature sounds are acceptable to everyone" is a reasonable inference from the absence of any objection, not a quoted ruling.

### Deaths, persecution and battles

Parents and authors split. Many children's books omit Bilal's torture and the battles; one father argues that this flattens the story and that hard truths are best met "gently... through the Seerah of the Prophet, as told to them by their own Father" ([MuslimMatters](https://muslimmatters.org/2012/06/26/seerah-and-your-kids/)). IslamQA's test, what the child "can relate to" and "according to her level of understanding", supports selecting and pacing, and it still names the campaigns as something to teach in time ([IslamQA 21215](https://islamqa.info/en/answers/21215)). Homeschoolers handle battles at about 8 as maps, fact cards and quizzes ([TeacherMomPlus3](https://teachermomplus3.blogspot.com/2023)). No educator or child-psychology source was found on how to tell under-8s specifically about the deaths of Abdullah, Aminah and Abd al-Muttalib. The staging that follows from the sources (state each death plainly and briefly, then go straight to who looked after him next; name torture and battle deaths only from about 6 to 8, told by the parent) is the researcher's inference, though Quranic Tarbiyah's printed practice at 5 to 6 matches it.

### The Sealed Nectar

The book won first prize among 171 manuscripts in the Muslim World League's 1979 competition and is arranged chronologically with sources named ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ar-Raheeq_Al-Makhtum)). No children's edition of it was found, so anything child-facing "based on The Sealed Nectar" is an original adaptation, and the book serves as the parent's reference for sequence and sourcing. Scholarly critiques of the book were not researched.

## Research backs repetition, questions and a parent in the room, and warns against free taps

The child-development evidence is strongest on five points and silent on several that matter to this app.

**Repetition works.** Three-year-olds read the same storybook three times in a week learned new words that children given three different stories with the same words did not ([Horst et al., PubMed 21713179](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21713179/)). A study of one Blue's Clues episode shown on five consecutive days to 3-to-5-year-olds found comprehension rose, attention held, and children answered and pointed more on later viewings; that study was seen only through secondary summaries ([SAGE citing article](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0002764204271505)). No study compares numbers of repeats, and nothing was found for ages 6 to 8.

**Questions with a pause work, including recorded ones.** Dialogic reading, where the adult stops to ask open questions, moderated word learning across 38 studies and 2,455 children ([Flack et al., PubMed 29595311](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29595311/)). Parents who paused story videos to ask questions beat parents who paused only to comment ([Strouse et al., PubMed 23544859](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23544859/)). For 58 four-year-olds, a pre-recorded reader who asked ten scripted questions and waited did as well as a live one, though small groups and a null result cannot prove equivalence ([Gaudreau et al., PubMed 33013552](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33013552/)). "Why" prompts helped 5-and-6-year-olds draw out and generalise a story's moral ([Walker & Lombrozo, PubMed 28010879](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28010879/)). Prediction questions have no direct test at 4 to 8; the one study found used 9-to-12-year-olds, and only those with better inhibitory control learned from wrong predictions ([Brod et al., PubMed 31626721](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31626721/)). No experiment was found on cliff-hangers, on how many questions suit a story, or on question timing for comprehension.

**Children miss the moral unless it is drawn out.** Children "represent stories at a concrete level" and extract morals late ([Walker & Lombrozo](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28010879/)). A classic study, seen here only in summary, found third-graders identified the intended theme about 10% of the time ([Narvaez et al.](https://nd.edu/~dnarvaez/documents/NarvaezMoralTheme99pub.pdf)). A story showing the good that came of honesty increased truth-telling in 3-to-7-year-olds, while stories about punishment for lying did not, and the effect vanished when the same story was rewritten around negative consequences ([Lee et al., PubMed 24928424](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24928424/)). Children generalise more from story worlds close to reality ([Walker et al., PubMed 25156667](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25156667/)). The finding that human characters teach better than animal ones has one positive study and one failed replication, so it is suggestive only. All behavioural effects were measured minutes after the story; nothing shows lasting change.

**Matching animation helps; things to tap hurt.** A meta-analysis of 43 studies and 2,147 children found animated pictures, music and sound effects that fit the story helped comprehension, while "interactive elements like hotspots, games, and dictionaries were found to be distracting" ([Takacs et al., PubMed 26640299](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26640299/)). Stories with multimedia only improved comprehension (g = 0.39) and stories that added interactive features did not, though "the more closely related the story and the interactive additions are, the smaller the cognitive cost" ([full text, PMC4647204](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4647204)). No study directly compares story-relevant with irrelevant hotspots at 4 to 8. The same analysis counts music among the helpful features, so leaving music out is a religious choice with a possible small cost, which story-matched natural sound is the obvious way to offset. Usability testing found tapping big targets easy for young children and dragging hard ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/children-ux-physical-development/)). On pace, nine minutes of a fast cartoon lowered 4-year-olds' executive function straight afterwards ([Lillard & Peterson, PubMed 21911349](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21911349/)), and the same lab's follow-up pointed to physically impossible events more than editing speed ([Lillard et al., PubMed 25822897](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25822897/)). These are immediate effects, mostly from one lab. Whether miracles shown on screen behave like cartoon fantasy is the researcher's extrapolation and untested.

**A parent present beats the same content alone.** Co-viewing is associated with stronger language across 12 studies (r = 0.16), a correlation ([Madigan et al., PubMed 32202633](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32202633/)). A study of 4-to-5-year-olds, seen only in summary, found children recalled some of an e-book from audio narration alone but most after reading with a parent ([Cooney Center](https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/2017/07/26/judging-a-book-by-more-than-its-cover-exploring-features-of-traditional-and-e-book-reading-experiences-that-support-childrens-learning/)). With toddlers, e-books cut parents' story talk and raised talk about operating the device ([Munzer et al., PubMed 30910918](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30910918/)). The WHO sets one hour of sedentary screen time as the ceiling at 3 to 4 and encourages "reading and storytelling with a caregiver" ([WHO](https://www.who.int/news/item/24-04-2019-to-grow-up-healthy-children-need-to-sit-less-and-play-more)). The AAP's 2026 statement moves from limits to design and context and warns about media that "prioritize engagement" ([PubMed 41556917](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41556917/)); the full statement was not read.

There is no research-validated number of minutes for a story at any age. An expert's 10 to 12 minutes of preschool read-aloud is opinion, and the article quoting it says "there's no set prescription from research" ([Education Week](https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/how-much-time-should-elementary-teachers-spend-reading-aloud/2026/11)). The suggestion of 3 to 6 minutes at 4, growing towards 10 to 15 at 6 to 8, is a design inference. On religious stories, 5-and-6-year-olds from practising families judge religious-story figures to be real ([Corriveau et al.](https://www.bu.edu/learninglab/files/2015/10/Corriveau-Chen-Harris-2015.pdf)), and by 9 to 10 Iranian children separate divine events from magic ([Davoodi et al., PubMed 35192175](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35192175/)). No controlled study was found on teaching faith narratives, seerah or Islamic story apps to this age; the Indonesian classroom studies of prophet stories are small, uncontrolled and were seen only in summary.

## Sixteen changes for the app, each tied to a finding

The recommendations assume the app as it stands in the repository: twelve stories from the Year of the Elephant to the first believers, five or six scenes of roughly 30 to 75 words each, objects that can be tapped at any time during the telling, a pilot story whose scenes end on a question answered by touch, and two questions and a weekly habit kept in the grown-ups' area. "Strength" says how firm the basis is.

| # | Area | Change | Finding it rests on | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Story selection | Add three or four stand-alone "meet him" stories for the 4-to-5 stage: his kindness to children and animals, what he looked like and loved, his camel. Check each against sound hadith first. | Guidance sources start with the person and what the child relates to ([IslamQA 21215](https://islamqa.info/en/answers/21215), [IIPH](https://blog.iiph.com/instilling-the-love-of-prophet-muhammad-sa-in-our-children)); Muis and Miraj use single gentle episodes at this age. The hadith were not verified in the research. | Agreed guidance; no trial |
| 2 | Story order | Keep the twelve in chronological order, but open the path with a person-centred story (Halimah or the birth) and let the Elephant come second or third. Keep the first pass at about 12 to 20 stories. | Chronological Makkan sets of 10 exist for 5-to-6 (Quranic Tarbiyah); first passes cluster near 20; the Elephant is about a king and an army, not about him. The reordering is this report's judgement. | Programme practice plus inference |
| 3 | Story length | Hold stories at five or six short scenes, about three to four minutes. Lengthen only for the 6-to-8 stage, and by adding stories before adding minutes. | Miraj runs 2 to 7 minutes for 4 to 9; no research sets a number; attention follows comprehension, not the clock. | Inference from practice |
| 4 | Repetition | Keep one new story a week and make replay the default: aim for at least three hearings of each story in its week. Do not unlock on first completion. | Three readings in a week and five daily viewings both helped ([Horst et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21713179/)); familiarity also raised belief in a story's reality. | Experiments at ages 3 to 5 |
| 5 | Question types | In the pilot, keep "find it and touch it" questions for turning the page, but stop relying on predictions ("what do you think it did?"). Never mark a guess wrong. Add one "why" question at the end of each story with a real silence and no touch needed. | "Why" prompts are tested at 5 to 6; predictions are untested at 4 to 8 and learning from wrong guesses needed older children's self-control. Recorded questions with a pause matched a live reader at 4. | Single studies |
| 6 | Parent's questions | Replace one of the two recall questions per story ("On which day was he born?") with a question about the child's own life ("Who looks after you?"). | Quranic Tarbiyah's recall, explain, apply-to-self pattern; transfer improves when the story meets the child's world; AboutIslam's "how do you think it felt?" technique. | Programme practice plus single studies |
| 7 | Explicit moral | End every story with the lesson in one plain sentence in the parent's voice, the week's habit spoken to the child, and a one-line du'a. Frame it as the good that came of the virtue. | Children miss themes unaided; explicit statement and self-explanation both help; positive consequences worked where punishment stories failed ([Lee et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24928424/)). | 8 | Interactions that distract | Switch off tapping while the voice is speaking. | 9 | Interactions that help | Roll out the pilot's pattern to all stories: narration stops, one large target that is the thing just spoken of, the tap moves the story on. | 11 | Parent involvement | Change the stated purpose from "use alone" to "listen together first, alone afterwards". Make the live "Read aloud" telling the first hearing of each new story. End each story with a card that hands a question to the parent, and let the child record a question of his own for the parent to answer. | Every programme assumes an adult tells; parent-shared reading beat narration alone; co-viewing r = 0.16; a recorded question cannot respond to the child. The question-recording feature is inference. | Meta-analysis (correlational) plus single studies |
| 12 | Parent's voice | Keep it, and treat it as second best to live telling. | 13 | Hands-on follow-up | Give each story one thing to do off the screen: a printable colouring page, a card to add to a wall timeline or map of Makkah, and the week's sunnah with praise when he does it. | Colouring, tracing, maps, timelines and crafts recur across programmes; WHO favours non-screen activity with a caregiver. | 14 | Deaths | Keep the three deaths, each in one plain sentence followed at once by who looked after him. Consider moving "he was very sad" towards "and Allah sent someone to love him". | 15 | Battles and persecution | Keep battles out until about 7 to 8, then teach them as maps, places and facts told by the parent. | 16 | Depiction and sound | Keep "no people drawn" and state it as the rule for the Prophet, his family and companions alike. Keep the star and the calligraphic name as they are, never a glowing human outline, and know that no ruling was found on such stand-ins. A voice-only refrain is open to you under every position read. | Drawing no one meets every position; music is disputed; all accept unaccompanied voice; story-matched sound aids comprehension. No fatwa on stand-ins or on sound effects was found. | Fatwa consensus on depiction; gap on stand-ins |

Two cautions sit across the table. The Urdu text and each story's facts need checking against the book before recording, because the fatwa literature's one firm condition on children's material is fidelity to sound sources, and the README already says the Urdu is unchecked. And the app should stay free of streaks, autoplay and rewards for time spent, in line with the AAP's concern about engagement-driven design; each story's fixed ending already gives a natural stop.

## Conclusion

The app's constraints turn out to be its strengths. Drawing no one and using no music were chosen for religious reasons, and they also produce the slow, plain, realistic screen that the pacing and distraction research favours. The risk lies elsewhere: an app good enough for a child to use alone invites the parent to step back, and the evidence says the parent in the room is worth more than any feature. Treating the recording as a rehearsal of the live telling, not a replacement for it, resolves that.

The parent is also in a position to answer questions nobody has studied. Whether his own recorded voice holds the child better than the tablet's, whether a story about someone never shown is followed, whether the "why" question at the end gets an answer, and what the child asks about the deaths are all observable at home within a few weeks. Since no programme has published what children of this age take from its seerah teaching, careful notes on one child's retellings would be more evidence than the field currently offers.
